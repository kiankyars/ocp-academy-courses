# pronunciation: LVDC = L V D C; OCP = O C P; AC = A C; DC = D C. Technical numbers and abbreviations are written for speech in the body.

Two voltage labels can look alike while describing different things. A voltage class establishes a scope category. A nominal voltage identifies a system family. A rated voltage belongs to equipment and its specified conditions. An operating range describes what a source delivers or what a load accepts during actual operation.

This distinction matters when reading the preferred voltage discussion in the paper. Values such as three hundred fifty, seven hundred, and fourteen hundred volts appear in a standardization context. They are not a substitute for the rating and behavior of the products in a proposed installation. The associated amendment work must be distinguished from a published requirement.

Notation also carries information. A slash can describe conductor-to-midpoint and pole-to-pole values in a bipolar arrangement. A plus-or-minus label describes the relationship of poles to a reference. Neither label by itself proves the voltage that an insulation system can experience relative to earth, particularly during a fault or a change in referencing.

For every voltage on a drawing, ask: between which two points, under which state, and for how long? Then identify whether the value is a nominal label, a product rating, or a required operating condition. That small discipline prevents a nominal label from being mistaken for a compatibility guarantee.
