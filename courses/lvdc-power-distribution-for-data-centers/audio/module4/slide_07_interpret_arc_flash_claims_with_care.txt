Electrical safety is not one number. Shock concerns current through a person. Series arcing can occur at a damaged or separating connection. Arc-flash incident energy describes a thermal exposure under stated assumptions, while arc blast includes pressure and mechanical effects. These are related hazards, but they are not interchangeable measures.

Faster fault clearing can reduce the duration of an event. That does not justify a universal claim that D C is safe, that a particular technology eliminates arc risk, or that protective equipment is unnecessary. The outcome depends on the available energy, geometry, working distance, enclosure, and the behavior of the protective system.

The paper discusses calculation approaches, including the maximum power method. A method should be read together with its applicability and assumptions. The worked values in one example are internally inconsistent, so this course does not reproduce a numerical incident-energy result from that example.

Instead, use the example to ask what a defensible study requires. Which fault or arc is being modeled? What source and load-side contributions are included? How is clearing time established? What physical arrangement and exposure distance are assumed? Which method applies to that installation?

The useful conclusion is that safety decisions require system-specific evidence. A generic fast-clearing example can illustrate a mechanism, but it cannot qualify the actual equipment arrangement or replace the assessment used for an installation's operating practices.
