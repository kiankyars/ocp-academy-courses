A fault is not supplied by a single ideal current source. Immediately after a low-impedance fault begins, charged capacitors can discharge into the fault path.

Cable and circuit inductance shape the rate of current rise. Converter controls and switching devices then influence the continuing contribution.

The load side may contribute too. Input capacitors and other connected energy sources can backfeed toward the fault, depending on the circuit and the devices between them.

Looking only at the upstream supply's steady-state current limit can therefore miss important parts of the event.

Different converters can be fault limiting or fault feeding under different conditions. A commanded current limit is not automatically the current seen during the first instant of a fault.

The relevant evidence includes the converter's actual transient response, stored energy, blocking behavior, and interaction with downstream protection.

The diagram separates these contributions without assigning universal current peaks or timing.

The source paper includes examples with conflicting numerical labels, so this course uses the qualitative mechanism instead of presenting those values as a validated design case.

For a protection study, identify every energy source that can reach the fault. Include the operating configurations that alter those paths, such as a bus tie, local storage, or another connected converter.

Then evaluate the current waveform and the device duties together. The fault response belongs to the complete connected system, not just the component nearest the fault.
