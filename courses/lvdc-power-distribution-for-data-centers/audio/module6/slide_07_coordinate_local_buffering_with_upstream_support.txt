A rapid rack load excursion can be much faster than the preferred response of the upstream supply. Local buffering can temporarily absorb that mismatch, reducing the abrupt change seen farther up the distribution chain. This is a specific control and energy-management role.

Do not equate local buffering with facility backup. The amount of stored energy, the power capability, and the loads inside the supported boundary may be very different. A rack buffer may support the computing input while leaving a required cooling or control dependency outside its reach.

Coordinate the buffer with the upstream converter and any wider bus storage. Determine who responds first, how support is handed over, and how the buffer is recharged without creating another unacceptable load step. State of charge, temperature, health, and converter limits can all affect availability of the intended function.

Protection must include the local energy source too. The buffer may contribute to a fault or leave energy present after an upstream switch opens. Its connection, isolation, and reporting functions therefore belong in the same interface review as the rack input.

The energy storage systems companion course provides further detail on deployment and acceptance evidence. Use that resource to deepen the storage review, while keeping the rack-level question precise: what event is the buffer meant to manage, which loads does it support, and what proves that it will do so under the stated conditions?
